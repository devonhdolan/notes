# Issue 224

Hello\! The motive is the mission. The why. It’s the story’s beating heart. It results in a the journey, usually ending in altruism. It applies to the characters in the narrative, and all those involved in making it. Usually it’s the first question I ask when I hear a pitch. Why do you want to tell this story or start this company? What’s the inspiration? The answer sells.  
Story is everlasting, style is everything.  
Both are needed for art to endure… One is the externalization — *the execution* — of the other.  
The death trap of familiarity is a trope, because it works. But the output is easily forgotten, disposability for commerciality. Compensation as motivation. Most in positions of influence want to invest in ‘the proven’ because they fit in a neat little box, an argument settled. The market movie with an action star, the cheap horror flick, or the superhero spectacle. They’re each their own brand, some nine circles of hell. It is known, it is okay. Mediocrity.  
If you work in any facet of entertainment, I’d encourage you to watch Michael Arndt’s [90 minute video essay on Story Endings](https://www.pandemoniuminc.com/endings-video). He wrote Little Miss Sunshine, Toy Story 3, and more. It should be required viewing before any story note is given or taken. The lecture is better than most film school classes combined. Thank you RJ for sharing this with me a few weeks ago, I think I first stumbled across it on the Lessons from the Screenplay YT channel — another treasure trove for film lovers, our favorite 20th century medium.  
That’s all for now. Thanks for reading. Remain curious. See ya next week.
