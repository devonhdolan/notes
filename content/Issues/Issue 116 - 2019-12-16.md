# Issue 116

Hello\! Here goes the quick rundown on newsletter metrics... and i*f you’re reading this it’s too late.* Same if you [smack this link](https://open.spotify.com/album/0ptlfJfwGTy0Yvrk14JK1I). It’s to Drake’s album of the same name on Spotify (:  
I wanted to do a brief writeup on my findings because when I made the switch to Substack from Mailchimp last month, I didn’t find much material online for either. And really, I didn’t have too many forces guiding my decision, though here are a few: I wanted to implement a paywall, have a sleek re-design at the ready, keep it American (sorry Revue), and support an independent and younger company (sorry Constant Contact, Mailchimp).  
The audience analytics and insights were an afterthought because I would still do this even if only my friends and family were the only people who read it, and I don’t want to get distracted by the metrics (opens and clicks). That notion kind of changed because I realized that sometimes I’ll plant a link and keep a keen eye on how it performs. Especially if I’m *discreetly* promoting something in the preamble that I might be involved in and/or seriously interested in. It’s usually relevant to what’s on my mind and in the news, and not an advertorial by any means. A guiding light if you will. This is the conversion rate. The one metric to rule them all. When I track this, I feel extraordinary nosy. Like a snoopy neighbor. No worse, a stalker. The open rate is a close second behind “guided link click thrus” because it doesn’t matter how many subscribers you have if they don’t want to engage with your content.  
But first, some general stuff you should know. All newsletter owners can see your email address and basic metrics (opens and clicks). Re-read that. If this is a concern, then you should use a dummy email address with RSS readers (Feedbin, Stoop), and/or setup a new email address under an alternate name or identity. Alias. Some of you use work emails (this isn’t good because you might change jobs). A handful of you asked me to add your personals after a job change and the paywall. I accommodate because I’m nice like that.  
From my side. I don’t change the title of these things (subject: issue \#) because that could influence and bait opens. I want to keep it clean. True to form. I rip out *all* affiliate links. There are A LOT as I collect the news from a bunch of random newsletters and publications: weeklies, dailies, and social media posts. I don’t understand how newsletters get caught and auto-deleted in spam filters and/or placed in promotions or junk tabs. I’ve had to forward my mom the last couple issues because of the former. No joke. I can roughly tell if you’re forwarding the newsletter based on how many devices have opened it, and how many opens there are. That’s fine. Finally, and most importantly, I would never share or sell this data. Your secrets are safe with me.  
Let’s get into it real quick.  
I like Substack, quite a bit actually. I chose it because their out of the box template was clean, simple, and user friendly. Same with setting up the paywall. The Stripe integration was easy enough. Mine just got mangled because I was doing the partner program on Medium at the same time \- for another day. They make mention of podcast and video publishing on their product roadmap. A lure for anyone who’s getting into written content creation. Hooked.  
And now, the bad. To un-subscribe is a multi-step process. It should be as seamless as subscribing. I can’t see which emails bounce so that I can remove them \- I’ve heard that this has an impact on how newsletters are filtered. Same note, if you un-sub it just says your email is “disabled”, but the email is still stored on my side. This makes it hard to purge inactive accounts. More so, it actually keeps them tallied as part of the audience on the dashboard. Bad. It has views, which is meaningless. Some times it will over-count the email open rate, full glitch. Mailchimp did this too. I think it’s a firewall spasming out. I presume it auto-cancels the paid subscription through Stripe \- most of you are comp’d anyway.  
The worst is that they organize and display audience information in an alphabetical order (conjoined with opens, clicks, or devices), not *chronologically*. This doesn’t make sense to me. In other words, Substack shows data on a user basis rather than an email basis. It’s better to see who open when, rather than how many times who opened an issue. This is where they should tear a page out of Mailchimp’s playbook. I switched from Mailchimp because their design tools are clunky and paywall wasn’t going to be super easy. That’s pretty much it. Looking back, I probably should have just figured it out rather that make the switch. No turning back now though. Perhaps I just grew accustomed to it. Habitualized.  
To be fair, Substack is the new kid on the block and their dashboard is still in beta. That said, Substack did [announce they raised \$15.3m](https://techcrunch.com/2019/07/16/substack-series-a/) from a16z and YC in July (\!), and they have only 6 people on the team \- Chris Best, the cofounder/ceo, said so [last week in a Tweet](https://twitter.com/cjgbest/status/1205370918609936384). They’re actually generating revenue too. What are they doing with all that capital? Hire (or contract) one or two engineers to bang out the metrics. Their latest announcement was that I can include a photo next my name \- cute, but who cares.  
Random thoughts. If the audience is paying for content, I’d imagine that opens and clicks would increase. Just a hunch. Also, I was thinking of how I could possibly just mass text a working link to the newsletter from Community or Superphone. Hmm.  
All this is to say, the online media business looks a hell of a lot different when you’re on this side of the table. Vox posted a great explainer on the subject [here](https://www.vox.com/recode/2019/12/10/20962868/vox-media-privacy-policy-explained-what-we-know-about-you). It’s why I sneer at so many armchair critics and analysts who claim they’re know-it-alls when it comes to the online media industry, but have never been one to actually hit “publish and send email.”  
That’s all for now. Thanks for reading. See you next week.
