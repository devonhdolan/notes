# Issue 63

​​Hello from early retirement. I quickly want to say thank you, to each and every one of you, for being a subscriber. All the congratulatory emails last week made me feel special, and better yet were the people who wanted to jump on a call to put our brains together on some projects. That was really cool. Okay, back to reality. Dynamic pricing is on my mind because 1\) it’s Cyber Monday, 2\) I just bought my int’l flight tickets, and 3\) individual public equities in the tech sector (read: my entire portfolio) have been going haywire. It’s only sensical that prices rise and fall given demand of a product or service. The unit measurement of this concept is called price elasticity of demand (thanks Google). I’m slightly obsessed with this idea because static prices, or fixed rates, seem to plague the media industry for one-off transactions (box office ticket sales and download to rent / own). This is one one of the few ways consumers directly transact with individual content, and I’ve got my eyes peeled to see if major media/tech cos will experiment here. A startup has little to no power playing around with dynamic pricing because the ordeal necessitates a seriously sizable audience in the first place… so this won’t work for a small, self-released indie film either. But, imagine if you could buy tickets to the new Lion King movie after the trailer dropped (which it did last week and generated 100s of millions of views), the price would’ve certainly gone bananas and spiked. Yes, subscription-based businesses have been eating away at this market, especially when they price their service at \$0.99/m in an effort to scale before setting actual price (hi Hulu), but I do believe that the home video/electronic sell-thru (EST) window can be maximized for value by using dynamic pricing, along with box office ticket sales (or not if there’s no demand). After the premiere, content is a bit of an anomaly because its owners can create a near infinite supply of its accessibility, but you can manipulate the market by artificially limiting links and prints. I firmly believe that the secondary markets should be squashed, like StubHub. Scalpers suck. This ideal could work for eventized content too, such as live sporting events pay-per-view (PPV) streams, including e-sports. Side note, the PPV of the Woods-Mickelson golf match was an absolute failure — how does that even happen? Double side note: Watching golf is torture for me. It’s dreadfully boring. In all, consumer behavior has been warped and trained to think that content has fixed price points, so I think it’s too late to change the game unless you’re offering one hell of an experience, cue Taylor Swift’s 2018 tour where she netted an extra \~\$50m for being dynamic. She’s such a boss. A final note to leave you with, it’s not a deal if you didn’t plan on buying the product or service in the first place. That’s all for now. See you next week\!
