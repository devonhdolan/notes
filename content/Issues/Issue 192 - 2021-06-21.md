# Issue 192

​​Hello\! It’s been a fun and eventful week at Tribeca, from New Zealand. The illustrious Kent Bye [said](https://twitter.com/kentbye/status/1405635507837497344) of our project, “Polæ is a polished, interactive sci-fi pilot with an amazing score & rich worldbuilding explored via an iOS app with speculative product ads, spatial models, wiki pages, driving news updates, AR, & cutting-edge ML GANs.”  
If you weren’t able to check out Polæ during its premiere run, you can write me direct (just reply) and we’ll invite people to the beta on a rolling basis. We’ve got grand ambitions to expand upon it :)  
Fun fact, [I was on](https://voicesofvr.com/292-the-four-different-types-of-stories-in-vr/) Kent’s Voices of VR podcast over five years ago to riff on immersive storytelling frameworks, about the theories I put to paper with my good friend Michael Parets [in TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2016/01/14/redefining-the-axiom-of-story-the-vr-and-360-video-complex/) — which I’ve been told is required reading for a 101 creative writing class at a top film school. Pretty neat.  
This all serves as precursor to say that I think a lot about the ways in which we tell stories, and how we can evolve them for the 21st century. For me, this goes hand-in-hand with how we monetize, market, and distribute them \- which is of the utmost importance. “If you build it, they will come” is pure fallacy.  
Story is everything.  
Now more than ever, it seems, development execs want something to point to *in the wild* \- be it a book, article, comic, or podcast \- as a deductive argument to say hey this has been published (or it’s going to be), therefore it’s validated and proven, so there must be a built-in following who’d recognize and watch it — even if the fanbase is measly, but mighty.  
One thing I’d encourage, is for creators (and curators) to have direct points of communication with their audience to bridge that final gap. Close the circuit, enable notifications. This is the greatest benefit of living in the age of the Internet. Dots, connected — converted. Play over a chord.  
Though audiences must be trained from the outset so they know what to expect, and when (generally). Like if I were to start serializing short stories in these emails, they probably wouldn’t catch on. I’d need to create a new dedicated home for that type of work. A channel, scheduled. Don’t tempt me.  
And once there’s *material* interest, a fervor, from the market for a specific story, the stage is set, the game must be played. People’s insecurities. One offer will likely beget others, thank you social proof. Catch lightning in the bottle.  
I’ve been asked aplenty about virtual production, digital avatars (as NFTs\*), and more. To me, these are just tools to help digitally streamline aspects of production for stories, told visually. The only tried and true tools you need are pen and paper. And writing is so hard. The hot tech topics might get you some coverage, but you need to capture someone’s imagination with an *actual* story. Nobody is stopping you. Hell, Soderbergh published a novella on Twitter in 2013 ([FastCo](https://www.fastcompany.com/3018506/steven-soderberghs-twitter-novella-is-now-a-hardcover-book-but-you-can-read-it-online)).  
I’m sure I’ve harped on all this stuff in past issues, I’m a broken record today. Write, and work, reductionally. That’s a made up word, but I like it.  
That’s all for now. Thanks for reading. Be a revisionist. See you next week.
