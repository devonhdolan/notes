# Issue 176

Hello\! I’ve had a couple fire drills this morning, and was galavanting around Queenstown over the weekend, so keeping this preamble light. I’ve been thinking heaps about PVOD and virtual screenings recently as we’re in the middle of a massive paradigm shift for the industry, and for consumer behavior/expectations. PVOD is one of the last straws of hope for filmmakers to capture “outsized” upside before studios self-deal on their own streaming services (when we instigating the Top 10 list bonus I pressed for in [issue 133](https://devonhdolan.substack.com/p/issue-133)?). Because release windows are truncating at a rapid clip, the box office pop will likely coincide with PVOD numbers. It’s a shame these figures are rarely, if ever, shared publicly. Curious to see how Antenna, Nielsen, Rentrak, and Parrot might unearth them. They should, and will, inform studio outputs with their SVODs, along with all other related transactional data.  
I’ve also been thinking about how talent, film/tv execs, and VCs, handle inbound as I’m constantly prepping pitches. A form of tone setting. The deck for a film/tv project is a sales tool to get them to spend an hour to read a script (which must win them over in the first 10 pages); and for startups, it’s for them to take the introductory meeting. Return potential in both businesses is wildly different. Netflix worked with Stage 32 for a pitch workshop a few weeks ago, I didn’t attend but there’s [this deck in a Dropbox link](https://www.dropbox.com/s/z88ax4zmhquaeeb/Netflix%20Pitch%20Workshop%20PDF.pdf?dl=0) that gives some rather obvious pointers. Knowing the other party’s tastes and mandate is the fastest way to a yes. Communicate the idea simply, conversationally. Make the project personal… special.  
Along these lines, I spend way too much time crafting emails because I’m a firm believer that every email will be forwarded \- up, down, across \- an organization. Probably down for an assistant/associate to read and review. You gotta be a word surgeon with lawyer logic, where every word will be deciphered according to the receiver’s agenda (the asst). And this might be the pessimist in me, or just me being auto-biographical, but air on the safe side and think that *most* people are generally dumb, lazy, and selfish. So if you can state the benefits of your pitch quickly, and get buy-in from the other side — over the decomposing bodies of ideas along the Maginot Line — then you’re set. Feed the receiver easy assists, make them look good, and constantly work ahead of the curve. Have fun with it.  
To top this thing off, here are a couple ninja tips and tricks to get further intel from the pitch, or the person sending it. First, play detective. People over-share on that open résumé site (LinkedIn) and that professional status games site (Twitter). Have a scroll through those before hitting send, try to occupy the mind-space of whoever’s receiving your pitch. Do a bit of investigating, of Googling, and tailor the pitch to the company’s agenda. For major tech companies, these are reported on all the time. Try to subtly repeat what the company owner might’ve said in the last month or two. Course, set.  
You can right click “inspect” on web pages if you’re trying to gather info on potential technology vendors a company uses. You can right click “get info” on PDF decks to see who actually prepped them. You can screenshot DocSends and merge the pages back into your own internal PDF, but know that DocSends are great for the sender for version control and knowing who actually reviews what, for how long. There are email checkers online (always try: firstname@website.com). You can review public SEC (Edgar), copyright, and trademark filings online to glean if there’s internal/external counsel. There are plenty more…  
That’s all for now. Thanks for reading. Credits are your currency. See you next week.
